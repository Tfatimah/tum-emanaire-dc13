From Answers to Possibilities

How Young Professionals Develop Problem-Framing and Judgement Skills Through Human–AI Collaboration

Independent proposed research · EMANAIRE DC13 · Technical University of Munich · Tasneem Fatima

1. Background and Research Motivation

Across spring and winter university intern intakes, I have mentored, trained and worked with students and fresh graduates entering professional life. Their interaction with digital tools has made me curious about whether iterative collaboration with generative AI changes not only their immediate output, but the way they approach and frame unfamiliar problems. Some young professionals begin with an imagined outcome and work backwards, using AI to generate alternatives, test constraints and refine their questions. This is a starting observation, not an assumed characteristic of an entire generation.

Research on critical thinking among knowledge workers suggests that generative AI may shift attention towards verification, integration and task stewardship (Lee et al., 2025). Research on human–AI co-creation also shows that repeated interaction alone does not guarantee improved joint creativity (Luan et al., 2025). What remains worth investigating is whether—and under what conditions—iterative AI-supported exploration develops transferable problem-framing and judgement skills for early-career professionals, distinct from the quality of AI-assisted outputs.

2. Research Aim and Questions

Central question: How does iterative human–AI collaboration shape young professionals’ ability to frame unfamiliar problems, explore alternatives and exercise independent professional judgement?

  1. How do young professionals frame and reframe work problems with AI, explore alternative approaches, and incorporate practical constraints?
  2. How do such interactions shape cognitive responsibility, assessment of AI contributions, and decisions about when human judgement should take precedence?
  3. How do these skills develop through repeated collaboration, and when do they transfer to new problems or settings with reduced AI support?

3. Conceptual Perspective

The project draws on three linked concepts: problem framing (identifying underlying needs, boundaries, assumptions and possibilities); human–AI co-agency (sharing exploration while maintaining human control and accountability); and professional capability development (distinguishing impressive assisted outcomes from skills a person can exercise in subsequent situations). It does not presume AI inherently strengthens or weakens thinking. The mechanisms and boundaries would be evaluated empirically.

4. Proposed Research Approach

I envision an exploratory mixed-method approach, refined collaboratively with the supervisory team. First, qualitative interviews and walkthroughs of AI-assisted tasks could identify how early-career workers construct prompts, adapt goals, evaluate uncertainty and select possibilities. Participants could help identify realistic workplace scenarios, consistent with youth participatory approaches (Cortesi, Hasse & Gasser, 2021).

Second, scenario-based behavioural tasks could compare how participants frame similar unfamiliar problems under different AI-support conditions. Measures could include problem definition, diversity and relevance of considered alternatives, recognition of assumptions, evaluation and delegation decisions, and justifications. Third, repeated or follow-up tasks could assess development and transfer when AI assistance is reduced. Professional experience, task complexity, mentoring and organisational expectations would be considered as contextual factors. Participants’ informed consent, confidentiality and responsible treatment of workplace data are essential.

5. Expected Contributions

Conceptual: clarify how AI-assisted problem-framing relates to co-agency and the development of professional judgement. Empirical: examine actual interaction practices and distinguish human reasoning from assisted performance. Practical: inform AI fluency and workplace learning activities that enable meaningful exploration without sacrificing responsible human decision-making.

6. Research Fit and Development

My interest arises from more than 12 years across enterprise technology, product management and organisational transformation, including AI-enabled workflows, product discovery, user research, and mentoring interns and early-career colleagues. My exploratory study When AI Thinks With Us has encouraged me to pursue more systematic investigation. As a first-generation university graduate whose learning was substantially self-directed, I also appreciate the significance of guidance, opportunity and independent judgement in professional development.

Prof. Sandra Cortesi’s work on young people's participation in digital environments is an especially valuable foundation. I would welcome the opportunity to refine these questions, conceptual boundaries, and methodological choices with Prof. Cortesi and the supervisory committee, allowing young professionals' own perspectives and empirical findings to shape the research.
